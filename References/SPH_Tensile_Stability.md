Detailed Summary: Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics Stability Analysis

Smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) is a gridless Lagrangian technique that is particularly attractive for modelling high-deformation events in computational mechanics. However, this study addresses a critical instability that occurs within SPH when subjected to tensile stress regimes. In experimental tests using the cubic B-spline kernel, when a small velocity perturbation of $$10^{-10}$$ km/s was applied to a single particle under tensile stress, particles rapidly clumped together and large voids formed within only $$100 \\mu s$$. In contrast, under compressive stress, no significant particle displacement was detected. This study demonstrates that this instability results in exponential growth of particle velocities, with the growth rate depending on the stress level, and that while the exponential growth eventually transitions to oscillatory motion, the artificial clumping of particles mimics physical fracture and fragmentation, making the numerical instability difficult to distinguish from real physical phenomena.

To understand the underlying cause of this instability, the authors employed von Neumann stability analysis, a rigorous mathematical technique for analysing perturbation behaviour in numerical schemes. The analysis simplifies the one-dimensional SPH equations to make the algebra tractable while preserving the essential physics. The key finding is that stability or instability depends on the sign of the product of the stress and the second derivative of the kernel function ($$\\sigma W''$$). Remarkably, there are no stress or strain thresholds for the onset of instability; it can occur even for infinitesimal perturbations. The instability criterion is independent of the artificial viscosity coefficients, indicating that increasing artificial viscosity cannot suppress the instability. The analysis shows that in standard SPH calculations using the cubic B-spline kernel with nearest-neighbor particles, instability typically occurs in tension, although instability in compression is theoretically possible with different kernel choices. For an arbitrary number of neighbours, stability depends on the sum of the kernel second derivatives at all particles with odd separations from a given particle within the smoothing length.

The physical mechanism driving the instability is fundamentally different from what might initially be expected. The smoothed particle hydrocode (SPH) equation of motion, when using kernel approximations, produces an effective stress proportional to $$-\\sigma W'$$, which differs from standard finite-difference formulations. When the constitutive stress-strain relation is multiplied by a function like $$-W'$$ that decreases as particles separate, the resulting effective stress-strain relation contains regions where effective stress decreases with increasing strain, producing a negative modulus and thus an imaginary sound speed. This imaginary sound speed is the root cause: changes in stress amplify rather than reduce changes in strain, making the system inherently unstable. Critically, this instability is not caused by the numerical time integration algorithm but rather stems directly from the interaction between the kernel approximation and the constitutive relation. At the shortest resolvable wavelength (the noise frequency), the effective stress becomes proportional only to the kernel function derivative and the stress sign. If the stress is tensile (positive $$\\sigma$$), the effective stress is proportional to $$-W'$$; where $$W'$$ has a negative slope, tensile stress decreases as particles separate, and increases as they approach, generating instability. The study also demonstrates that artificial viscosity cannot stabilise this condition because the growth factors remain real and positive even when viscous terms are added to the wave equation. Additionally, the authors note that various remedial schemes are theoretically possible, such as coupling an appropriately modified kernel with a variable smoothing length so that the system remains stable for the current stress sign, although practical implementation requires balancing stability requirements with the need for compact support in the kernel function.

A particularly striking insight from this work is the complete decoupling of the instability mechanism from temporal discretisation schemes; the instability is a property of the continuous SPH differential equations themselves, not an artefact of how time is approximated numerically. This implies that no choice of time integration method, however sophisticated, can remedy the fundamental instability when an effective negative modulus exists. Furthermore, the paper reveals that particle clumping, which appears physically meaningful, is actually a numerical artefact: particles naturally congregate into configurations that satisfy the instability criterion, creating structures that superficially resemble physical fracture and fragmentation. The criterion for instability, which depends solely on whether $$\\sigma W'' > 0$$ or $$\\sigma W'' < 0$$, is remarkably elegant and threshold-free, operating at all strain levels. The work also establishes that the frequency dependence of the instability explains why, even though the criterion contains no explicit threshold, instability manifests predominantly in high-frequency perturbations (the noise frequency), allowing for some stability in very small perturbations when the kernel geometry is precisely arranged. These insights collectively demonstrate that SPH's stability limitations arise from fundamental mathematical properties of the kernel approximation method rather than implementation details, opening pathways for kernel design modifications to enhance stability while maintaining accuracy and compact support requirements.
